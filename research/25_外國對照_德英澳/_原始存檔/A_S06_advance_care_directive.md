## 工具回傳文字（原樣）
Each state and territory regulates advance care planning differently, so the answer depends on where the person lives. I ran one search, and the sources are mixed in age and in places they disagree, so check current legislation before relying on any of this.

**New South Wales:** NSW is the outlier in having no statutory form for future health care choices. Every state and territory except NSW has a statutory form for future health care choices. One older source says that in NSW the use of Advance Care Directives is encouraged and such directives appear to be acted upon within the health care system, though it is not mandated by specific legislation. Another source describes NSW directives as persuasive but not legally enforceable in jurisdictions relying on common law recognition. Note that the NSW position is contested across sources.

**Victoria:** Victoria has both kinds of directive. Advance Care Directives are regulated by the Medical Treatment Planning and Decisions Act 2016 (Vic). A person may create both a common law Advance Care Directive that refuses treatment and/or a statutory Advance Care Directive, and both types of Directives are recognised by the law in Victoria. Its directives will only apply when the person loses capacity and cannot make a decision about that medical treatment. In Victoria, an Advance Care Directive must be signed by two independent adults, including one registered medical practitioner.

**Queensland:** The document is called an 'Advance Health Directive'. Only statutory advance care directives are legally binding in Queensland. Decision-making without capacity runs through the Powers of Attorney Act 1998. Under certain circumstances the Queensland Civil and Administrative Tribunal (QCAT) can cancel an advance care directive or attorney appointment.

**Substitute decision-makers:** Every state and territory have a statutory form for appointing a substitute decision-maker. The rules on this are complicated, and the sources don't fully agree on how far a directive from one state is honoured in another. One source says that common law advance care directives from other states and territories are not recognised in Queensland. Another says that a valid Queensland advance care directive will apply in other states and territories in Australia, although there may be some limitations and additional requirements. Given this conflict, get advice before relying on an out-of-state document.
