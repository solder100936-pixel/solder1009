## 回傳文字（原樣）
I found no Taiwan survey that reports the exact percentages you asked about, broken down by oncologists and nurses. The closest data are below.

- **Closest numbers are from mainland China, not Taiwan.** A survey of 180 physicians in Shandong Province found that 98% of the physicians would discuss the cancer diagnosis with family members before discussing it with the patient, and 82% of them will not tell the patient if the family requests "not to tell". It also found that nearly half of physicians would want to withhold the diagnosis if the patient were a member of their own family.

- **Taiwan palliative care survey (2002).** This nationwide survey of 229 palliative care workers at 15 hospices was mostly nurses: 72.5% were nursing staff, and only 38 respondents were physicians (16.6%). It reports that families always request that medical professionals do not disclose the truth about the diagnoses and prognoses of the cancers to the patients. The snippet doesn't give the share of respondents who reported such requests.

- **Taiwan physician interviews.** A qualitative study of 17 attending physicians at two medical centers identified family requests to conceal the truth as one of four main themes. Physicians should judge based on the patient's disease condition. Another summary notes that most physicians cooperate with family members to determine the level and method of disclosing unfavorable news to patients.

- **Patient and caregiver views.** A study of 617 patient-caregiver pairs across 21 Taiwanese hospitals found that cancer patients strongly asserted their superior rights to be informed about their disease over their family. The authors concluded that physicians need to respect patients' preferences rather than routinely taking the family's opinions into consideration first in the event of disagreement.

For Taiwan-specific percentages by physician specialty and nursing role, you would likely need the full text of the 2002 Cancer Nursing paper or the qualitative study's publication.
