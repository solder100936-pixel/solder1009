## 工具回傳文字（原樣）
The search turned up a mix of developer statements, player reactions, and scholarly critique of Auti-Sim, a 2013 indie game about childhood sensory overload.

**Critiques from autistic players:** Some autistic players said the game did not reflect their own experience. There has been some negative feedback too, including messages from some autistic people who said the game is nothing like their experience. The developers acknowledged that autism's breadth makes a single representation impossible. As a result of autism's wide spectrum, it was impossible to create one game that represented all possible experiences.

**Scholarly critique:** Markus Spöhrer (University of Tübingen, 2023) argues the game is problematic. He says it reduces the autistic spectrum to hypersensitivity, and that its restricted player choices suggest that fleeing is the only way out of a painful situation. He argues that assuming every social situation with some noise level is like a "horror game" for every autistic person reduces the spectrum to hypersensitivity. He also notes that the game removes the coping strategies people use, such as putting on noise-cancelling headphones.

**Empathy framing:** Players often praised the game as an empathy exercise, and the developer framed it as awareness-raising. Many player responses to Auti-Sim on Game Jolt concern the instructive value of the experience as an exercise in empathy. The developer, Taylan Kay, said it's more of a statement on how bad it can be rather than how it is for everyone.

**Design critique:** A First Person Scholar essay argues that the game lacks clear objectives and is hard to call enjoyable, which raises the question of whether it is a game at all. Like many simulation games, and open world or sandbox games, it lacks a cohesive narrative structure and distinct player objectives.

The results didn't cover the broader "empathy games" debate in depth, so I can't say how Auti-Sim fits into that critique specifically.
