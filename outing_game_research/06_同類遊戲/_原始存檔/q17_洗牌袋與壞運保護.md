## 工具回傳文字（原樣）
The search turned up practical design discussion, though little formal research on perceived fairness.

- **Shuffle bags:** A forum post on Dungeon of the Endless describes the method: items are drawn from a bag and removed, so they're less likely to repeat until the bag empties and resets. The same poster notes the bag has to be large enough to keep outcomes unpredictable but small enough to prevent long runs of bad luck. (Then you generate a random number between 1-number of items in your shuffle bag and pull out that item in the list to use. Then you remove that item from the shuffle bag.)
- **Bad luck protection:** One source is a Medium article, "Designing Fair and Fun Randomness in Video Games via Bad Luck Protection," with code on GitHub, but I only saw its title and not its content. A minifiniti.pages.dev guide describes pity mechanics as a safety net after a losing streak. (Pity mechanics can guarantee a positive outcome after a streak of bad luck, giving players a safety net.)
- **Streaks and fun:** A Game Developer post reports that players lost interest when streaks of bad luck hit, and recommends pairing randomness with choices players control. (Streaks of bad luck or bad luck at bad times can cause your players to get fed up with the system, so it's important to balance RNG with player choice.)
- **Deck versus dice:** Quarriors uses colored dice bags to add variation to dice draws, and a Slay the Spire commenter suggested shuffle mechanics to soften bad luck streaks with cards. I found no controlled study comparing perceived fairness of decks and dice.

Gaps: the results don't cover pseudo-random distribution (the formal name for the weighted-probability approach behind many bad luck protection systems) or any user research on perceived fairness. The Medium article and the Tutsplus shuffle bag tutorial would be the best next sources to check.
