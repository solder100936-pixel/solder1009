## 工具回傳文字（原樣）
The study you're asking about is Nario-Redmond, Gospodinov, and Cobb's 2017 paper "Crip for a Day," in *Rehabilitation Psychology*. The search results don't mention the "hostile, benevolent, and ambivalent" framing, so I can't confirm that link.

**Design:** Experiment 1 was a field study. Undergraduate students were engaged in simulations of dyslexia, hearing impairment, or mobility impairment in a public campus space. Experiment 2 moved into the lab, where undergraduates (N = 50) with and without disabilities each completed low vision, hearing impairment, and dyslexia simulations.

**Findings:**
- Empathy rose, but attitudes toward interaction did not improve. Empathetic concern (warmth) toward disabled people increased in both studies, but attitudes about interacting did not improve.
- Negative mood increased. Post-simulation anxiety, embarrassment, and helplessness were highest for those who used wheelchairs or simulated dyslexia.
- Pity and self-perceived competence shifted. In Experiment 2, participants judged themselves less competent, expressed more pity, expressed more interaction discomfort, and were not more willing to interview disabled students for an accessibility project afterward.
- Behavior did not change. Participants were no more likely to volunteer to improve campus accessibility and they expressed greater discomfort with interactions with people who have disability.

**Authors' interpretation:** The authors argue that simulations focusing on early experiences of impairment, without addressing social and cultural barriers and discrimination, serve to increase negative emotions towards people with disability. Their conclusion is that simulation promotes distress and fails to improve attitudes toward disabled people.
