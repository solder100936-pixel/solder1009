The search didn't turn up a 2020 systematic review, but it did return several useful sources on the debate and on alternatives.

**The critique of simulation:** Sally French's 1992 critique is the foundational text. It argues that disability equality training led by disabled people works better than simulation French's article, "Simulation Exercises in Disability Awareness Training: A Critique," published in Disability, Handicap & Society (French, 1992). In this work, French argues that rather than using simulations to foster understanding of the disability experience, a more effective approach would be disability equality training led by experts or persons with disabilities themselves. A 2004 paper by Burgstahler and Doe reviews whether the use of disability-related simulations is ever appropriate, and a 2019 policy brief notes that participants in disability and inclusion trainings can still glean meaningful lessons without imitating disability.

**Research evidence is mixed:** One study found that awareness interventions involving simulated disability could positively influence children's attitudes toward peers with visual impairments. Another found that a brief blindness simulation influenced how people without visual impairments negatively judged the capability of people with visual impairments.

**Social model framing:** The critique rests on the view that simulation comes from the Medical Model of Disability, which views the individual with an impairment as the problem. The alternatives below follow from the social model, which locates the problem in the environment.

**Alternatives identified:**
- **Panels and lived-experience speakers:** DO-IT recommends hosting a panel of individuals with disabilities who can share their personal experiences and make direct connections with an audience.
- **Disabled-led organisations and case studies:** One trainer suggests going to disabled people's organisations (which are run by disabled people rather than charities) for case studies.
- **Video:** Useful when disabled people cannot attend in person; showing videos of real individuals with disabilities sharing their experiences, including challenges as well as solutions is one option.
- **Conversation with disabled people:** Art Blaser's suggestion is to listen to a disabled person, one in your neighborhood, your class, around school, and ask them about their life rather than the medical aspects of the disability.
- **Partnership and co-design:** One practitioner argues for real change, we need a partnership model where disabled people's expertise and lived experience is actively sought.

**Caveat:** A 2025-era architecture education study notes that critics, including disability activists, argue that it may oversimplify the experiences of disabled people. It also reports the counterargument that design decisions should be driven not only by legal requirements but also by a sincere commitment to accessibility, which supports simulation's defenders.
