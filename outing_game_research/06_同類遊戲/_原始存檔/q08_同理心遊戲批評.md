## 工具回傳文字（原樣）
Anna Anthropy and Bo Ruberg are the two central voices here, and they critique the "empathy game" label from different angles.

**Anthropy's objections.** She has said she has been both an empathy game pioneer and one of the genre's staunchest critics. Her main objection is that the label lets players feel they have understood a marginalized experience too easily. She argues that the label "empathy game" gives players implicit permission to excuse themselves from doing ongoing work on their privilege. She also says empathy games rarely address how a privileged audience is complicit in the suffering they're dipping their toes into. Her response was an art installation, *Empathy Game*, which she describes as highlighting "hollow notions" of empathy and allyship won in a few minutes of gameplay. Her view is that Dys4ia was made for trans and questioning players, not cis audiences, so reading it as a tool for teaching cis people empathy is to claim it for cis audiences.

**Ruberg's critique.** Ruberg's 2020 article "Empathy and Its Alternatives" in *Communication, Culture & Critique* is the scholarly anchor. Per a summary, Ruberg's discussion is mainly framed around creators who resist the label, and Ruberg wants to put to bed the "empathy" label, but still wants to see a discussion of alternative emotional modes in games. A related critique is "empathy tourism," which Ruberg describes as a risk where complex forms of queer life are compressed into consumable emotions and moral reassurance. Ruberg also notes that playing at being queer can become a form of consumption rather than a catalyst for social change.

**Related threads.** A 2025 paper in *Paidia* extends this critique into therapy-oriented "empathy games" for autistic people, arguing from critical disability studies and feminist critiques of empathy. Its framing is that these games aim to build the very ability to empathize rather than to foster empathy for someone else.

**Caveats.** I did not access Ruberg's full text, so the points above come from secondary summaries and news coverage. I also did not search for responses from design scholars or defenders of the genre, so the case for empathy games is underrepresented here.
