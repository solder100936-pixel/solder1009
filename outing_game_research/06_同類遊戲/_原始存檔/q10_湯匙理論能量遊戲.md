## 工具回傳文字（原樣）
I ran one search and found a range of spoon-theory games. They mostly fall into three design patterns: daily energy budgets, tile puzzles, and cooperative tabletop play.

- **Daily energy budget (text or simple sims):** The Spoon Theory Demonstration is a short text-based RPG that starts the player's day with 20 spoons, or 20 measures of energy. Task choices raise or lower that spoon count. TidyHomebound is a time management strategy game where the player manages everyday household chores.
- **Energy-meter survival:** Robin, a point-and-click game about Chronic Fatigue Syndrome, uses an energy meter that depletes after any action, and when it's empty, the character must sleep. Its designer said spoon theory influenced the game's energy-based mechanics.
- **Tile puzzle:** One design student's prototype is a puzzle game where limited turns, represented by spoons, are used to collect colored tiles representing mental, physical, and emotional energy to meet daily objectives. The designer also tried to balance evoking the frustration of disabled people with keeping the game fun.
- **Cooperative tabletop:** Spoonies is a co-op game for 2-6 players, played over four rounds, each representing a week. Its win conditions are tiered (Thrived, Persisted, and Survived) rather than binary win/lose.

Design lessons that recur across these sources:
- Some designers frame energy as a visible meter, which players already understand from games. One critic noted that heads-up displays make unseen variables like health and ammo easy to read as finite.
- Accessibility of the game for the player matters. Spoonies includes dyslexia-friendly fonts and high-contrast visuals.
- Some games frame the goal as self-compassion rather than efficiency. One game aims to teach that it is okay to have limitations, to not be able to do everything, and to get help when needed.

The results don't include formal studies measuring whether these games change player attitudes or understanding, so evidence for their effectiveness as serious games is still anecdotal here.
