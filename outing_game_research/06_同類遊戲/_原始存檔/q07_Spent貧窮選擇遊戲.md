The search turned up good background on SPENT, though no formal evaluation of its effectiveness.

**What it is:** SPENT is a browser and mobile game about poverty and homelessness. McKinney, a Durham ad agency, designed it for Urban Ministries of Durham, which donated its services. It is an immersive experience that challenges players to survive poverty and see firsthand that homelessness is just a simple shortfall away.

**Design:** Players start out homeless and unemployed and must make hard choices to get through a month on $1,000. Players start out homeless and unemployed, and are forced to make tough choices to survive the month on a thousand dollars. The scenarios were built from focus groups with caseworkers and homeless people at Urban Ministries. The real-life situations were based on details raised in focus groups that the advertising agency conducted among the case workers and homeless people from Urban Ministries of Durham. The game also uses real local figures, such as Durham's fair market rent values to determine the game's housing prices.

**What players learn:** The game shows how fast employment, housing, medical, or other expense changes can cause serious harm. Throughout the game's one-month period, players learn how quickly changes in employment, housing, medical costs and other expenses can have disastrous consequences. Its designers also acknowledged limits: a 30-day window means the disasters it models would rarely all happen in one month, though Nelson said she has seen many happen within three months. The game's 30-day time frame makes Spent a little less than 100 percent realistic. All the disaster-generating scenarios players encounter generally wouldn't occur within a month. "But I've seen many happen within three months," Nelson says.

**Outcomes and reach:** Reported figures for plays vary by source. One report says the interactive game SPENT has been played more than 4 million times in over 218 countries. An earlier 2011 figure was over 1.7 million times, in 196 countries, according to McKinney. Urban Ministries also reported awareness gains: a surge of visits to their website, an increase in volunteers and new donations totaling over $45,000. These come from the organization and its agency, so they are self-reported.

**Evaluation gap:** None of the results I found is an independent or controlled study of whether players actually come away with better understanding of poverty or change their attitudes. The evidence is mostly the designers' accounts, press coverage, and a few reviews. Any claim that the game teaches players effectively would need a separate study.
