## 工具回傳文字（原樣）
The 2015 paper is Arielle Silverman, Jason Gwinn, and Leaf Van Boven's "Stumbling in Their Shoes," published in *Social Psychological and Personality Science* (vol. 6, no. 4, pp. 464–471). The authors argue that experience simulations shrink the perceived adaptability of disability and lower how capable disabled people are judged to be.

- **Experiments:** In one study, participants who simulated blindness judged blind people as less capable of work and independent living than participants who simulated another impairment or had no impairment. In another, blindfolded participants said they would be less capable if they became blind and would adjust more slowly than non-blindfolded participants.
- **Interpretation:** Silverman told reporters that people who imagine blindness take their own brief, superficial experience and assume life would be terrible and that they couldn't function well.
- **Broader context:** Silverman's later review in the *Journal of Blindness Innovation and Research* concluded that simulations promote empathy but can also promote discrimination.

Note that the press coverage I found is from the University of Colorado Boulder release and secondary outlets. I didn't access the full journal article, so the details above come from abstracts and news summaries.
