## 工具回傳文字（原樣）
I ran one search. It didn't return anything from the Reeve Foundation, so the points below come from ventilator-user, ALS/MND, manufacturer, and airline sources.

- **Battery planning:** Airlines often ask that batteries last about 1.5 times the flight length, which leaves room for delays or diversions. Rules on batteries can vary between airlines, so check in advance. Most will ask that batteries last one and a half times the flight duration. The extra power allows for delays or diversions. Test the battery well ahead of time, since a battery's claimed runtime should be verified far in advance.
- **Airline approval and paperwork:** The airline's medical and engineering departments may need to approve in-flight ventilator use. Provide the ventilator name, model number, and specifications, along with the manufacturer's contact details. Airlines typically have medical forms for your physician to complete. For United, you must bring a completed and printed copy of the onboard medical oxygen request form. Also confirm with your manufacturer that the device is intended for air travel (check with your ventilator's manufacturer).
- **Manual resuscitation bag:** One source recommends carrying a ventilator, battery, an ambu bag for manual ventilation when necessary, suction machine, and spare materials as hand luggage.
- **Storage and transfers:** Many planes allow the ventilator to be stored under the seat. If you change planes or airlines, tell the new airline too and confirm it has approved in-flight ventilator use.
- **Power wheelchairs:** A portable ventilator can plug into a power wheelchair battery if needed.

Policies differ by airline and change over time, so confirm current requirements directly with your airline and equipment provider. I didn't find Reeve Foundation-specific guidance, so a targeted search on their site or a spinal cord injury community would be the next step.
