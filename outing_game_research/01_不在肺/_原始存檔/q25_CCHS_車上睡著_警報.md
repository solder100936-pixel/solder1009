## 工具回傳文字（原樣）
The search didn't turn up travel guidance written specifically for CCHS children in car seats, so the points below are drawn from related sources.

**CCHS-specific findings**
- A 2020 survey abstract found that most children with CCHS did not wake to ventilator or monitor alarms, and most had no nighttime nursing. Of 23 respondents, 13 used pulse oximetry alone and 9 used pulse oximetry plus end-tidal CO2 monitoring. 17/23 rarely woke up due to ventilator or monitor alarms. Conclusion Most subjects with CCHS did not awaken to ventilator or monitoring alarms and a majority of these patients did not have nighttime nursing. This suggests that alarms alone may not protect a sleeping child during a car trip.
- Pediatric home guidance recommends that children requiring chronic home invasive ventilation be monitored with a pulse oximeter, especially when asleep or unobserved, rather than a cardiorespiratory monitor or sole use of ventilator alarms.
- Sleep-study schedules are relevant to travel planning: an annual polysomnography to evaluate ventilator support settings is recommended for older children, and for children less than 3 years of age, a sleep study every 6 months is recommended.
- Discharge guidance also suggests families be taught the importance of safe transport of the child with all necessary travel items in a "Go Bag."

**Car seat and monitoring findings (mostly preterm-infant sources)**
- Car seat challenge testing targets preterm infants, and it is not a CCHS protocol. Still, it shows that semi-reclined positioning may increase the risk of oxygen desaturation, apnea, or bradycardia in pre-term and low birth weight infants.
- A hospital protocol treats a child as unsafe to travel in a car restraint if they cannot maintain saturation above 90% for more than 10% of the assessment timeframe. Its additional criteria include bradycardia after repositioning below 100 bpm, apnea longer than 20 seconds, or persistent laboured respirations despite repositioning.
- Capnography can add information beyond oximetry. One dental-sedation study noted that pulse oximetry gives no early warning of airway obstruction or hypoventilation, while capnography could give advance warning of potential respiratory problems.

**Practical takeaway:** Ask the child's sleep team whether a portable pulse oximeter (and capnography, if already used at home) should run during car rides, and whether a car seat trial with monitoring is advisable before a long trip. Those decisions should come from the child's pulmonologist or sleep physician, since the search didn't identify a CCHS-specific car travel standard.
