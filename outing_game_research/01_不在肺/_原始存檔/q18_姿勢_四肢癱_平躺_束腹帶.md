## 工具回傳文字（原樣）
The search covered the tetraplegia half of your question well, but returned nothing on ALS, orthopnea, or diaphragm weakness, so that part remains unanswered.

**Supine vs. sitting in tetraplegia:** A 2003 study of ten people with cervical injuries found forced vital capacity was higher lying down than seated, and a binder raised it when seated (Values of forced vital capacity were higher in the supine than in the seated position.). A 1977 study reported FVC about 300 ml higher supine or in Trendelenburg than sitting (Forner et al. (1977) studied 20 patients with C4–8 tetraplegia and found that the mean value of the forced vital capacity was 300ml higher). A review attributes the supine gain to abdominal contents pressing the diaphragm upward, which allows greater diaphragm excursion (the weight of the abdominal contents providing support and elevation to the diaphragm).

**Binder effect:** One source describes the binder as keeping abdominal contents from falling forward and exerting traction on the diaphragm (An abdominal binder acts to keep the abdominal contents from falling forward and exerts a traction effect on the diaphragm.). Another study of seven patients found both binder types improved VC when seated or tilted to 70 degrees (Both binders improved VC in the seated position and at 70 degrees tilt, and sniff Pdi at 70 degrees tilt.). Evidence is mixed: a crossover study found an average 31% FVC gain with a binder, but one participant dropped 18% (5/6 participants demonstrated a mean increase of 31% in forced vital capacity with binder compared to without, which was not significant but this may be because the sixth participant showed an 18% decrease). That study was in paraplegia, not tetraplegia.

**ALS and orthopnea:** The results contained no ALS, orthopnea, or diaphragm-weakness sources, so I can't confirm whether the supine-vs-sitting pattern carries over to ALS. A separate search would be needed for that.
