The search turned up mostly clinical therapy protocols and educational pages. I found nothing that specifically addresses respiratory muscle fatigue, so that part of your question is unanswered here. Also, several sources are study guides or patient-education blogs rather than formal guidelines.

- **Exercise dosing:** A University of Michigan ALS therapy protocol suggests that moderate programs, roughly 30 minutes daily, appear safe for early-stage patients. It recommends moderate resistance and repetitions so muscle groups don't become over-fatigued, and it tells clinicians to watch for fatigue that lingers past about 30 minutes after exercise Moderate exercise programs (including light resistance exercises and aerobic exercise) for about 30 minutes per day is safe and does not accelerate disease progression. Programs should be focused on moderate resistance and moderate repetition to prevent over-fatigue of muscle groups. ... post-exercise fatigue should not last more than 30 minutes or negatively impact daily task performance. In later stages, the same protocol advises against exercising muscles below antigravity strength and against high-resistance work Avoid exercising muscle groups with less than anti-gravity strength.
- **Overwork warnings:** A study-guide summary of neuromuscular teaching describes the concern about exhaustive exercise worsening muscle function in unstable progressive conditions, and recommends low-resistance, non-fatiguing aerobic work with frequent rests Prescribe low-resistance, non-fatiguing aerobic exercise with frequent rest intervals. This is secondary material, so verify it against primary guidance.
- **Energy conservation:** Occupational therapy typically uses the "4 Ps" (prioritize, plan, pace, position) to protect function occupational therapy emphasizes the '4 Ps' of energy conservation: Prioritize, Plan, Pace, and Position. A randomized trial in motor neuron disease reported better mid-term fatigue management and occupational performance with energy conservation training, but no improvement in quality of life. using energy conservation strategies could lead to better mid-term fatigue management and occupational performance improvement, but it did not improve QOL in patients with MND.
- **Pacing expectations:** One patient-education source notes that pacing does not build strength; its benefit is reducing fatigue swings Energy pacing does not increase muscle strength; its measurable benefit is reducing boom-and-bust fatigue.
- **Breathing:** The ALS Association's exercise materials list breathing support among key fatigue-management pieces, alongside sleep and nutrition. Sleep, nutrition, and breathing support play very important roles. For respiratory muscle fatigue specifically, you'd need a pulmonary or neuromuscular source; none of these results covers it.
