## 工具回傳文字（原樣）
The search covered the 2008 Levine study on diaphragm atrophy, but it did not return anything on ICU-acquired weakness incidence, so I can't give you an incidence figure from these results.
**The Levine 2008 study:** Researchers compared diaphragm biopsies from 14 brain-dead organ donors with those from 8 control patients. The donors had been ventilated for roughly 18 to 69 hours, while controls had only 2 to 3 hours. Fiber cross-sectional areas were 57% smaller in slow-twitch fibers and 53% smaller in fast-twitch fibers in the donors (decreased cross-sectional areas of slow-twitch and fast-twitch fibers of 57% (P=0.001) and 53% (P=0.01), respectively). Glutathione was also lower, and caspase-3 expression was higher. The authors suggested the findings could have clinical significance, and noted that muscle atrophy in the diaphragm is a major contributor of why patients who have undergone prolonged mechanical ventilation often have difficulty breathing after being removed from the ventilator.
**Caveats:** Commentators pointed out the study's limits. One reviewer noted that given the trend towards lighter sedation and daily awakening, even patients receiving "full support" for ARDS are exhibiting some spontaneous breaths. That reviewer questioned whether the findings apply to current practice. The study was also small, and the donor subjects were brain-dead, which limits direct extrapolation to living ICU patients.
For ICU-acquired weakness incidence, you'd need a separate search, such as on ICU-acquired weakness prevalence in mechanically ventilated cohorts.
