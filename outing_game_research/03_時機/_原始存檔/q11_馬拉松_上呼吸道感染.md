## 工具回傳文字（原樣）
The search turned up the core numbers for the 1987 Los Angeles Marathon study (published by Nieman and colleagues in 1990), though the sources differ slightly on framing.
- **Odds ratio:** Nieman's later review reports that 12.9% of the marathoners reported sickness, compared with only 2.2% of control runners who did not participate (odds ratio 5.9). A 1990 secondary account gives the same rates and says the marathon participants were six times as likely to be affected, which matches the 5.9 figure roughly.
- **Study design:** The review describes the sample as 2,311 marathon runners who varied widely in running ability and training habits. Another source describes the outcome as one in seven marathon runners reported an episode of URTI during the week following the March 1987, Los Angeles Marathon, compared to two in 100 who did not compete.
- **Training dose:** Runners training .96 km/wk doubled their odds for sickness compared with those training ,32 km/wk. The text is garbled in the extraction, so the exact km/wk thresholds should be checked against the original.
- **Separate 1990 paper:** The "Infectious episodes in runners before and after the Los Angeles Marathon" article (J Sports Med Phys Fitness, 1990) reports a different measure. It found in runners training ≥97 vs <32 km/wk, the odds ratio (OR) for IE during the 2 month period prior to the LAM was 2.0, which is a pre-race finding rather than the post-race 5.9.
The post-race odds ratio of 5.9 is the figure most often cited for the week after the race. I didn't find the full-text article itself, so the exact confidence interval for 5.9 isn't confirmed here.
