## 工具回傳文字（原樣）
The search turned up no single study that measures accidental decannulation and death rates specifically for children on home mechanical ventilation. The closest findings come from smaller studies, mostly hospital-based, with a few home-care series.
- **Factors linked to accidental decannulation:** A 2023 Respiratory Care case-control study of 41 cases and 82 controls found the risk was higher in children who could reach their body midline while sitting, in those with a tube of 4.0 mm or smaller, and when staffing was lower. The study took place in a long-term care facility, not at home. The authors identified 41 cases and 82 controls between 2013–2018. The commentary on it notes that the study could not calculate the incidence of accidental decannulation because of its retrospective design. It also reported no deaths from accidental decannulation, which the commentary attributes partly to skilled staff.
- **Home care series:** An Indian Pediatrics report on 12 children sent home with tracheostomies found accidental tube displacement at a rate of 1.28 events per 1000 home tracheostomy days. The authors judged home care feasible with proper parent training.
- **Mortality at home:** A 2021 Frontiers in Pediatrics study of tracheostomy-dependent children found that 45 (27%) patients died during the study period. Pulmonary indications were associated with earlier death. A separate Indian Journal of Pediatrics report describes a child who had blocked tubes at home and died.
Overall, the evidence is limited, mostly retrospective, and uses inconsistent denominators, so it does not give a reliable death rate for accidental decannulation at home. Searching for prospective cohort data on home ventilation in children, or for the 2022 Laryngoscope prospective cohort on inpatient decannulations, would be the logical next step.
